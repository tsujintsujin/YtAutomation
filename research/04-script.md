## The short version
- **The topic and the first 30 seconds decide most of it.** The hit and the miss on the same channel use the same
  structure, voice and look. What differs: the hit opens on an extreme, concrete fact with a paradox and a stake for the
  viewer; the miss opens on "In 1998, five young men started a company".
- **One central question, asked in the first minute and answered at the end.** Every section answers a smaller
  question and hands over to the next one.
- **A story needs a turn**: one decision, a conflict, a low point, a comeback. Lists of facts without a turn are what
  viewers skip.
- **Explain with a tiny world**: a village, an ATM in a bank lobby, with the same few characters coming back.
- **Concrete beats general**: exact numbers made human-sized, dates, names, real quotes, odd details. Viewers rewatch
  the vivid scenes and skip the exposition.

## 4.1 What separates the hit from the miss (same channel, same format)
**Fortune & Fall: Labubu 2.1M vs Tencent 1.2K.**
- Labubu's first 30 s: a 4-foot toy sells for ~$150,000; the company was worth more than the makers of Barbie,
  Transformers and Hello Kitty combined; six months later the secret figure lost 88%; and the mystery: no competitor
  did it, **the company did it to itself with one decision any textbook would recommend**. Extreme fact, reversal, a
  question you need answered.
- Tencent's first 30 s: a founding date, five young men, a chat app copy. No stake, no question, no reversal. The
  middle is a list of purchases (Riot, Epic, Supercell, Ubisoft...) with the conflict only arriving at 6:15.

**PENNY: "$40 Trillion" 1.1M vs "Where Money Comes From" 8.1K.**
- Both use the same village, the same characters (Mia, Hank), the same chapters and a "3 things to remember" end.
- The hit opens with a number made human ("spend $500 a second since Jesus was born, still not enough"), a surprise
  (America's debt was zero once, in 1835), a crash that followed, and **"the real answer might include you"**. Three
  questions are promised; "by the end you'll be able to answer all three yourself".
- The miss opens with "what is a dollar actually worth?": a fine question with no conflict, no news and no stake.

**[mine] Lesson:** the same script craft lifts a strong topic to 1-2M and cannot save a flat one. Pick topics with a
big concrete number, a paradox or reversal, conflict between people, and a stake for the viewer; then write the open
around exactly those.

## 4.2 Story shapes that work
**[creator]** Jack Craig's arcs (his Shorts channels, matching what we see in long videos):
- **Linear:** hook → rising action → payoff. Predictable; fine for a single idea.
- **Conflict arc:** hook → rising action → **conflict → comeback** → rising action → payoff. "Less predictable, more
  engagement."
- **Looped arc:** two conflicts and two comebacks.

How the hits use them:
- **Person story** (Wolf, Anthropic): a cold open with the paradox ("he lied, got rich, served 22 months, and people
  still cheer him"), then origins with a wound or a hunger (Dario's father died a few years before a cure arrived),
  the rise, the conflict with a named person (dialogue with the boss), the walk-out, **the low point** (the investor's
  money was fraud money and ChatGPT launched the same month), the comeback, a second conflict (the Pentagon), a
  reversal (users rally), an open ending framed as a debate, a closing line.
- **Rise and fall of a company** (Labubu): rise → **why it worked** (three layers: blind box, scarcity, resale) → **the
  one decision** (make more) → the fall in numbers → "what now" (their plan) → "our view" (3 lessons, labeled as
  opinion).
- **Explainer** (PENNY $40T): three questions → a tiny village explains each mechanism → the real numbers after each
  village scene → a history case (Jackson 1835) → the other side ("both sides have a point") → three warning signs
  the viewer can watch → the answers, recapped.
- **What-if** (Ifverse): concrete senses first (you catch a fly, a falling cup) → the one rule (your brain is faster,
  your muscles are not) → escalating demonstrations (daily life → games → sports → national team) → the cost (false
  starts, boredom) → **a moral choice at the climax** (the ball or the ball boy) → reversal (lost the final, won the
  crowd) → a cliffhanger tease → a question for the comments.

## 4.3 The long-video template (12-16 min, ~2,000-2,700 words)
Timings from the hits (146-189 words a minute for money explainers):

| Part | Time | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Cold open | 0:00-0:30 | The most extreme concrete fact, made human-sized; the reversal or paradox; why it touches the viewer |
| Promise | 0:30-1:00 | The central question (or 3); "by the end you'll know..."; what the story will follow |
| (Subscribe line) | ~1:00 | One line with a reason ("this channel breaks down how money works inside...") [Fortune & Fall] |
| Section 1-2 | 1:00-5:00 | Origins / the mechanism, through a tiny-world analogy and its characters, then the real numbers |
| The turn | ~40-60% | One decision, a conflict, a betrayal or a crash; things go wrong |
| Low point | | The moment it looks lost; a character asks the question the viewer is thinking |
| Comeback / consequences | | What happened next, in exact numbers and dates |
| Payoff | last 2-3 min | Answer every promised question plainly; a takeaway the viewer can use (warning signs, a rule) |
| Close | last 20-30 s | Callback to the opening object or character; one closing line; the next video, set up by a thread from this one |

Rules inside every section:
- **Each section answers one smaller question and ends by asking the next one** ("So who holds all those IOUs?").
- **Something new every 20-40 s:** a number, a reveal, a line of dialogue, a joke, a scene change.
- **Callbacks:** introduce an object or a character early and bring it back (Hank's IOU, the ATM in the lobby, Mia's
  last dollar). The ending lands on it.
- **Label opinion as opinion** ("our view, not a fact"): trust, and it is also what YouTube's policy reads as the
  creator's own perspective (01, 1.3).
- **Name sources in the narration** ("according to Reuters", "the Bank of England explained it in 2014").

## 4.4 Delivery: how a topic is told
- **A tiny world for every mechanism.** PENNY's village (mayor, Hank, Mia, IOUs as coins), Fortune & Fall's bank
  lobby ATM vs the vault. Same characters across videos make a channel recognizable.
- **Short dialogue lines.** Characters speak in one or two lines, often funny ("I already have 47 pots."). In the
  Wolf video's replay data the peaks sit on dialogue and spectacle scenes.
- **Show the number, then shrink it.** "$40.4 trillion" → "$118,000 for every American, every newborn too" → "every
  extra 1% of interest costs ~$950 per person".
- **Real people, real quotes, exact dates.** Every hit is full of them; it is also the value YouTube's review looks for.
- **Give the other side.** "Some say disaster, others say relax; both have a point." Then the evidence.
- **Give the viewer something to do.** Three warning signs, a rule of thumb, a question for the comments.

## 4.5 What viewers rewatch and skip (YouTube "most replayed", Wolf of Wall Street, 4.3M)
**[YouTube data, read with yt-dlp]** Peaks (relative, 1.0 = most replayed): the opening (0.87); the teenage
"lemon ice" dialogue (0.6-0.67); the start of chapter 2 (0.91) and of the later chapters (0.39-0.48), where viewers
land when they jump from the chapter list; **the office party spectacle (1.00)**; the cash smuggled by a courier in
her clothes (0.83). Dips (0.03-0.13): the middle of a long sales monologue, "on
paper the firm was thriving" exposition, the FBI investigation summary, and the moralizing ending.
**[mine]** Vivid, specific, strange scenes and dialogue get rewatched; summary and exposition get skipped. Cut any
exposition block that runs over ~40 s without a new fact, scene or line of dialogue. (Replay data shows rewatching,
not drop-off; our own videos' retention graphs in Studio are the real test.)

## 4.6 Wording rules
- **Write for the ear.** Short sentences. One idea per sentence. Plain words ("IOU", "promise", "coins") before the
  real term, then the real term once.
- **Speed:** money explainers 146-189 words a minute; what-if stories ~105 (more pictures, more pauses). Our
  Chatterbox voice reads ~187 (laundromat-hobby: 2,578 words in 13.7 min), at the fast end: a 14-minute video is
  ~2,600 words.
- **Turns are said out loud:** "But here's the strange part." "Here's what most people miss." "And yet."
- **Cause, not sequence:** link beats with "but" and "so", not "and then". Each beat should cause the next.
- **Questions to the viewer:** "Would you hand it over?" "What would you do?" Then answer.
- **"You" in every explanation:** the viewer's bank account, their mortgage, their savings.
- **Concrete over adjectives:** "$30,000 of Labubus stolen from a warehouse in Chino" beats "the craze got out of hand".
- **Numbers rounded for the ear** ("about 1 in 5 tax dollars"), exact where the exactness is the point ("4:20 and 59
  seconds").

## 4.7 The Shorts script (30-50 s)
**[creator]** Jack Craig: sudden movement in the very first frame; one idea executed fully; 1-3 s cuts; sound effects
and music; story shape still hook → rising action → conflict → comeback → payoff. His "stayed to watch" target is ~80%;
his first Short got 61%, his second 80%+ (LuckySticks' best is ~30%).
**[mine, from the hits]** A Short is the one jaw-drop fact or moment of a long video, told complete: the fact as the
first line (no intro), why it's true in 2-3 beats, a twist, and a last line that sends to the long video or loops
back to the first line. Never a trailer that stops mid-story.

## 4.8 Measuring the script
**[YouTube]** Studio's audience retention report shows the share still watching at 30 seconds ("intro"), and the
"top moments" where almost no one left. YouTube's advice when the intro loses people: change the first 30 seconds, or
make the title and thumbnail match the video better. Data takes 1-2 days.
**[report]** MrBeast's leaked production guide: the first minute loses the most viewers; minutes 1-3 and 3-6 are the
next stretches to protect.

## 4.9 Decisions this part asks for
1. Adopt the template in 4.3 and the rules in 4.4-4.7 as `RULES.md` for the new channel (script checklist).
2. Every topic is checked for: big concrete number, reversal or paradox, conflict between people, stake for the
   viewer. A topic with none of them is dropped, however interesting.
3. A recurring tiny world and 2-3 recurring characters for the channel.
4. Read each video's 30-second intro and retention dips in Studio; rewrite the next script's open accordingly.
