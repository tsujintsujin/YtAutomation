Why do cats love boxes so much? You buy your cat a fancy new bed and it ignores it completely. Then it climbs straight into the cardboard box the bed came in. So what is it about a box that a cat simply cannot resist?

A parcel arrives at your door on a quiet afternoon. You carry it inside and set it down on the floor. You open it up and lift out whatever you ordered. Maybe it is a lamp or a pair of shoes or a nice new cat tree. And before you can even fold the flaps down there is a cat inside the box.

It does not matter how big the box is. It does not matter if the cat barely fits. A huge cat will happily squeeze into a tiny shoe box with its sides bulging out. It will sit there looking very pleased with itself. And the expensive thing that came in the box sits forgotten on the floor.

People joke about it all the time. You can spend a fortune on toys and the cat would rather have the packaging. But there are real reasons behind this. Some of them go back thousands of years and some of them are about how a cat feels inside. Let's start with the oldest one.

Your house cat comes from a small wild cat that lived in deserts and dry grasslands. That wild cat was a hunter. But it was also small enough to be hunted itself. Foxes and jackals and birds of prey and bigger cats would all have been happy to eat it. So a small wild cat needed two things at once. It needed a way to hunt and it needed a way to stay hidden.

The answer for both was a good hiding spot. A gap between rocks or a hollow under a bush or an old burrow in the ground. Somewhere small and dark with walls on every side. From in there the cat could rest without anything sneaking up behind it. And it could watch the world outside without being seen.

Cats are what scientists call ambush hunters. They do not chase prey for miles like wolves do. They wait. They hide somewhere quiet and stay perfectly still. Then when a mouse wanders close enough they spring out in one fast pounce. A good hiding spot is the most important tool an ambush hunter has.

Now look at a cardboard box from the cat's point of view. It has walls on every side. It has one opening to look out of. It is just big enough for one cat. It is basically the perfect hunting hide. Your cat sits in there with only its eyes and ears showing. It feels safe and it feels ready. Anything that walks past is about to get ambushed. Sometimes that something is your ankle.

The second big reason is stress. Cats are animals that like things to stay the same. They like the same food and the same routine and the same quiet places. When something changes they can get anxious. A new house or a new pet or a loud party can all upset a cat. And when a cat feels stressed its first instinct is usually to hide.

Scientists have actually tested this. In 2014 a team at Utrecht University in the Netherlands studied cats that had just arrived at an animal shelter. A shelter is a very stressful place for a cat. Everything is new and noisy and smells of strangers. Some of the new cats were given a box to hide in. The others were not.

The cats with boxes settled in much faster. Within a few days they were calmer and less stressed than the cats without boxes. They got used to their new home and to the people around them more quickly. Something as simple as a cardboard box made a real difference.

So a box is not just a toy for a cat. It is a safe room. When the world gets too loud the cat can go inside and shut it all out. In there nothing can come at it from behind or from the side. It only has to keep an eye on one small opening. For an anxious animal that is a huge relief.

This is also why cats love other small spaces. Think of all the odd places you have found your cat. Inside a drawer. Under the bed. In the bathroom sink. Inside a laundry basket. Inside a paper shopping bag. Wedged on a shelf between two rows of books. All of those spots have one thing in common. They wrap around the cat and they make it feel tucked in and protected.

The third reason is warmth. Cats like to be warmer than we do. Scientists talk about something called the thermoneutral zone. That is the range of temperatures where an animal does not have to spend any energy to keep warm or to cool down. For people that zone sits around room temperature. For cats it is much higher. It is roughly between thirty and thirty eight degrees Celsius. That is about eighty six to one hundred degrees Fahrenheit.

Most of our homes are a good deal cooler than that. So a cat in an ordinary living room is often a little bit chilly. That is why cats chase sunny patches across the floor all day. It is why they sleep on top of warm laptops and right next to radiators. And it is one more reason they love boxes.

Cardboard is a surprisingly good insulator. It traps the warmth of the cat's body inside the box. A small box holds that heat even better because there is less air to warm up. So when your cat squeezes into a box that looks far too small it might know exactly what it is doing. It has found itself a cozy little heater.

The fourth reason is that boxes are new and interesting. A cat's world is pretty small when it lives indoors. It knows every corner of your home and every smell in it. So when a box arrives it is a big event. It has come from somewhere else. It smells of trucks and warehouses and the people who handled it. A cat simply has to go and check it out.

And once it has checked it out the cat often wants to claim it. Cats have scent glands on their cheeks and their chins. When a cat rubs its face along the edge of a box it is leaving its smell behind. It is quietly marking the box as its own. Some cats also love to scratch and chew the cardboard. Scratching leaves more scent from glands in the paws. It also feels great on their claws. A cardboard box is a hiding spot and a scratching post and a chew toy all in one.

Now here is the strangest part of the whole story. Cats do not even need a real box.

You may have seen a trend online where people put a square of tape on the floor. Just four lines of tape making an empty square. And very often the cat walks over and sits right in the middle of it. It is as if the cat thinks that square is a box.

In 2021 a researcher named Gabriella Smith decided to test this properly. She ran a study with the help of cat owners at home. The owners set up shapes on the floor and recorded what their cats did. One of those shapes was a real square made of tape. Another was a famous optical illusion called the Kanizsa square.

The Kanizsa square does not actually have any sides. It is just four shapes that look like little round pac men. They sit in the four corners with their mouths pointing inward. Your brain fills in the missing lines and you see a square that is not really there.

The cats in the study sat in that illusion square about as often as they sat in the real one. That suggests cats might see the same illusion that we do. Their brains fill in the edges too. And it means even the idea of a box is enough for a cat. The internet already had a name for this. If it fits I sits.

Big cats love boxes too by the way. Sanctuaries that care for lions and tigers have filmed them with giant cardboard boxes. The huge cats pounce on them and squeeze into them and tear them apart just like a house cat would. The instinct runs through the whole cat family.

So should you give your cat boxes? Absolutely. A cardboard box is one of the cheapest and best gifts you can give a cat. Just check it first. Take out any staples and long strips of tape. Remove any plastic bags or loose ties. And cut off any handles that a cat might get its head stuck in.

If your cat seems stressed it is a good idea to give it more than one hiding spot around the home. A box in a quiet corner can help a nervous cat a lot. This is especially true after a move or when there are guests over. You can even cut a doorway in the side of a box and add a soft blanket inside. Some people turn a pile of boxes into a little cardboard castle.

There is one thing to watch for though. Hiding is normal but a big change in hiding is worth noticing. If your cat suddenly hides all the time and stops eating or stops using its litter box it may be unwell. Cats often hide when they are sick or in pain. So if the hiding comes with other changes it is a good idea to call your vet.

So why do cats love boxes so much?

Because they come from small wild cats that needed safe hiding spots to survive. Because a box is the perfect place for an ambush hunter to lie in wait. Because walls on every side make an anxious cat feel safe. Because cardboard keeps a cat warm in a home that feels a bit chilly to them. Because a new box is full of new smells and fresh cardboard to scratch. And because a cat brain seems to love the idea of a box so much that even a square of tape will do.

So the next time a parcel arrives leave the box out for a while. Watch your cat climb in and turn around and settle down. It looks silly to us. But to your cat that box is a hunting hide and a safe room and a warm bed all at once. And honestly that is a much better present than whatever came inside it.
