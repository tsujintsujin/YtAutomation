Why does your cat knock things off the table? You watch it happen and it looks almost like a plan. The paw reaches out and the cup slides toward the edge. So what is really going on inside that little head?

It is late in the evening and you have just made a cup of tea. You set it down on the coffee table and turn away for one second. When you look back the cat is sitting right beside the mug. It is not drinking the tea and it is not sniffing it. It is just looking at you. Then a single paw comes up and rests on the side of the mug. Slowly the mug starts to slide toward the edge. The cat is still looking right at you the whole time. And then the mug goes over the side.

If you live with a cat you have almost certainly seen some version of this. Pens roll off desks. Keys vanish under the sofa. Earrings disappear from the bedside table. Plants end up on the floor with soil everywhere. The whole internet is full of videos of cats doing exactly this. And in nearly every one the cat looks completely calm about it.

So is your cat being a little menace on purpose? Or is something else going on? The answer turns out to be several things at once. Some of it is very old and some of it is about you. Let's start with the oldest part.

Your house cat comes from a small wild cat that lived in the deserts and grasslands of Africa and the Middle East. That wild cat lived on mice and birds and lizards and bugs. To stay alive it had to hunt a lot. A small cat cannot catch one big meal and rest for a week. It needs many small meals every single day. So it spends a big part of its life looking for things that move.

And here is the thing about small prey. Sometimes a mouse plays dead. Sometimes a lizard freezes perfectly still. So a hunting cat learns a very useful trick. When it finds something that might be alive it gives it a poke with its paw. If the thing runs then dinner is on. If it does not move then the cat pokes it again just to be sure.

That paw tap is one of the most basic moves a cat has. You can see it in kittens long before they ever catch anything. They bat at bits of string and leaves and their own brothers and sisters. They are practicing the moves they will need later. The poke and the swat are wired deep into every cat.

Now picture your cat on the kitchen counter. It finds a small object it has never seen before. Maybe it is a bottle cap or a hair tie or your car keys. The hunter part of its brain asks a very simple question. Is this thing alive? And the quickest way to find out is to give it a tap.

The object slides a little. That is interesting. So the cat taps it again. It slides a bit further and then it falls off the edge. That is even more interesting. Something moved and then it went somewhere. For a hunter that is about as exciting as life gets.

There is another reason cats use their paws so much. Cats do not see very well up close. Their eyes are built for spotting movement across a room or a field. Things that are right under their nose tend to look a bit blurry. So for close work a cat leans on other senses.

Their paws are packed with sensitive nerves. Their whiskers can feel tiny changes in the air around them. Cats even have a few stiff whiskers on the backs of their front legs. Those help them feel what is happening under their own chin. So when your cat pats at something on the table it is partly just getting a better look. It is feeling the object out to learn what it is.

There is also some evidence that cats are trying to figure out how the world works. In 2016 a team of researchers at Kyoto University in Japan ran a clever little test. They shook a closed box in front of cats. Sometimes the box rattled as if there was something inside it. Sometimes it made no sound at all. Then they tipped the box over. Sometimes an object fell out and sometimes nothing did.

The cats stared longer when what happened did not match the sound. A rattling box that dropped nothing seemed to puzzle them. A silent box that suddenly dropped something did too. The researchers took this as a sign that cats understand some cause and effect. They seem to have a rough idea that shaking means something is inside and that things fall down.

So maybe your cat is running its own experiments. What happens if I push this pen? It falls. What about this spoon? It falls too and makes a lovely noise. Does the remote control fall? Yes it does. Every test comes back with the same answer and yet it never seems to get boring.

But the hunting instinct does not explain the most famous part. It does not explain the look. Why does the cat stare at you while it pushes the mug toward the edge?

This is the part that is all about you.

Cats are very good at learning what gets a reaction. Think about what happens the first time a cat knocks something off a shelf. You jump up from the sofa. You say its name in a loud voice. You rush over and pick the thing up. Maybe you even pick up the cat. For a few seconds you are paying total attention to it.

From the cat's side that is a great result. It was bored and now you are up and moving and talking. It does not really matter to the cat that you are cross. Attention is attention. So the cat remembers that pushing things off the table makes the human come running. And next time it wants you it knows exactly which button to press.

That is why so many cats do this when they want something specific. It happens a lot around mealtimes. The cat walks along the counter in the early morning. It looks at you lying in bed. Then it knocks your phone onto the floor. That is not a random accident. That is a cat that has learned how to get you out of bed and into the kitchen.

It also happens when you are busy with something else. Have you noticed that cats love to knock things off your desk while you are working? You are staring at a screen and ignoring them. So they walk across the keyboard or push your pen off the edge. Suddenly you are looking at them again. Mission accomplished.

Some experts also point to plain boredom. An indoor cat has a hunter's body and a hunter's brain. But a lot of indoor cats have very little to hunt. They sleep and eat and look out of the window. Knocking things over is fun and it uses all of those hunting moves. If a cat does not get enough play it will invent its own games. And your belongings make very convenient toys.

Young cats tend to do this more than old ones. A kitten or a young adult cat has a huge amount of energy. A senior cat often prefers a sunny windowsill and a long nap. So if your young cat is clearing every shelf in the house it may simply need more to do.

Sometimes there is an even simpler reason. The cat wants to be where the object is. Cats like to lie on tables and shelves and desks. If your book is in the way the cat may just push it aside to make room. In that case the falling part is not the point at all. It is more like tidying up in a very cat sort of way.

So how do you stop it? Or at least how do you make it happen less?

The first rule is not to punish your cat for it. Yelling at a cat or spraying it with water does not teach it that knocking things over is wrong. It just teaches the cat that you can be scary. And in some cases the yelling is the reward the cat was looking for anyway.

The easiest fix is the boring one. Put the precious things away. Keep glasses and mugs away from the edge of the table. Put breakable things inside a cabinet. If there is nothing fragile to knock over then a knocked over object is no big deal. Some people use a little museum putty to stick ornaments down so they cannot move.

The second fix is to give your cat a better hunt. Play with it every day using a wand toy with feathers or a toy mouse on a string. Let it stalk and chase and pounce. Let it catch the toy at the end so it feels like a win. Many cat experts suggest playing right before a meal. That way the cat gets to hunt and then eat just like a wild cat would. A cat that has had a good hunt is usually much happier to curl up and sleep.

Puzzle feeders help a lot too. These are toys that hold dry food or treats. The cat has to bat and roll and paw at them to get the food out. That turns all that swatting energy into something useful. Some cats will happily spend ages working a treat ball across the floor.

You can also give your cat its own shelf with its own things on it. A few safe toys on a low shelf can become the cat's private knocking spot. If something falls off there it does not matter.

And when your cat does push something over try not to make a big scene. Stay calm and pick it up later. If you rush over every single time the cat learns that it works. If nothing exciting happens the game gets less fun. Then make sure you give your cat plenty of attention at other times. Give it attention when it is calm and gentle and not just when it causes trouble.

There is one more thing worth saying. A cat that suddenly starts knocking things over far more than usual might be trying to tell you something. It could be bored after a change in your routine. It could be hungry because its food changed. Older cats sometimes act differently when their eyesight starts to fade. If the change is big and sudden it does no harm to mention it to your vet.

So why does your cat knock things off the table?

Because it is a hunter at heart and a quick tap is how hunters check if something is alive. Because its eyes are not great up close and its paws help it explore. Because it seems curious about how things fall and what sound they make. Because it has learned that a falling object makes you jump up and pay attention. And because sometimes it is just bored and your stuff makes a fun toy.

So the next time a paw slides your mug toward the edge take a moment before you shout. Look at the cat looking back at you. It is not being evil. It is a tiny desert hunter stuck in a living room. And right now it really just wants you to come and play.
