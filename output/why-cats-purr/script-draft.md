Why does your cat purr? Most people will tell you that a purr means a happy cat. But cats also purr at the vet and when they are badly hurt. So what is that purr really telling you?

You are lying in bed on a slow Sunday morning. The cat climbs up and settles right on your chest. Within a few seconds the little motor starts. It is a soft and steady rumble that you feel more than you hear. Your whole chest hums along with it. The cat closes its eyes and the room feels a little calmer.

It is one of the nicest sounds in the world. It is also one of the strangest. For a very long time nobody could even say how a cat makes it. And the reasons a cat purrs turn out to be far more interesting than just being happy.

So let's start at the very beginning. Let's start with a box full of newborn kittens.

A kitten is born blind and deaf. Its eyes stay shut for about the first week or two. Its ears are folded down and closed. It cannot walk and it cannot keep itself warm. All it can really do is crawl toward its mother and eat.

Now think about the problem that gives the mother. She has a pile of tiny kittens that cannot see or hear her. How does she let them know that she is close? And how do they tell her that they are doing fine?

The answer is the purr. Kittens start purring when they are only a few days old. The mother purrs right back at them while they nurse. A deaf kitten cannot hear much at all. But it can feel a vibration through its whole body. The purr works like a quiet signal between them. The mother is saying that she is right here. The kittens are saying that all is well.

There is a clever detail in this too. A kitten cannot meow while it is drinking milk. Its mouth is far too busy. But it can purr with its mouth full. So the purr lets a kitten talk to its mother while it eats.

So purring is one of the very first things a cat ever does. It belongs to the warmest and safest days of its life. And just like kneading it never really goes away.

When your grown cat purrs on your lap it is using its oldest language. Many experts think it is treating you a little like its mother. You are warm and soft and you bring the food. From where the cat is sitting that makes a pretty good mother.

But how does a cat actually make the sound? That question puzzled scientists for a surprisingly long time.

Some of the early ideas were rather wild. One old theory said the purr came from blood rushing through a big vein in the chest. Another said cats had a second set of vocal cords just for purring. None of those ideas held up.

For years the best answer was all about muscles. Inside a cat's throat is the voice box. When a cat purrs the muscles around the voice box twitch very fast. They twitch around twenty to thirty times every single second. Each twitch opens and closes the gap between the vocal cords. The air going through gets chopped into a fast and steady rumble. And the cat does this while it breathes in and while it breathes out. That is why a purr can go on and on without a break.

Most cat sounds only happen on the way out. A meow is made with air going out. A hiss is made with air going out. The purr is special because it keeps going in both directions. If you listen very closely you can sometimes hear it change as the cat breathes.

Then in 2023 a team of scientists found a twist. They studied the voice boxes of cats that had died of illness. They found that a voice box could make a purring sound with no twitching muscles at all. Just blowing air through it was enough. Inside the vocal cords they found small pads of soft tissue. Those pads make the cords heavier so they can shake very slowly. That is how such a small animal can make such a deep sound.

So the full answer is probably a mix of both. The brain and the muscles start the purr and keep it going. The special build of the voice box gives it that deep and steady rumble. Scientists are still working out the details. It is a little amazing that one of the most familiar sounds in your home is still a bit of a mystery.

Here is another fun fact about purring. Not every cat can do it.

Your house cat purrs. So does a cheetah. So does a puma and a lynx and a bobcat. But the big famous cats are different. Lions and tigers and leopards and jaguars cannot purr the way your cat does. Those four are the cats that can roar.

For a long time people thought it came down to a small bone in the throat. In the roaring cats part of that bone is soft and stretchy. That was supposed to explain why they roar instead of purr. Today the story looks more complicated. The shape of their vocal cords seems to matter a lot too. A lion can make a purring sort of sound when it breathes out. But it cannot keep the rumble going in and out like your cat does.

So in the cat family there is a rough rule. Most cats can either purr or roar. Very few can really do both. Your little cat got the purr. And honestly that is the better deal for a lap.

People have loved this sound for a very long time. The English word purr was first written down in the 1600s. It was made to sound just like the noise itself. Other languages did the same thing. In French a cat goes ronron. In German a happy cat will schnurren. In Japanese the purr is goro goro. Everywhere people live with cats they found a word for that rumble.

Now let's get to the real puzzle. If a purr means a happy cat then why do cats purr when they are miserable?

Ask any vet and they will tell you the same thing. Cats purr on the exam table. They purr when they are scared. They purr when they are injured and in pain. Mother cats purr while they are giving birth. And some cats purr quietly when they are very old and close to the end.

None of those are happy moments. So the purr cannot simply mean that a cat is content.

Many experts now think of the purr as a way for a cat to calm itself down. It is a little like a person humming when they are nervous. Or rocking back and forth when something hurts. The steady rhythm seems to soothe the cat. Some scientists think purring may even help the body release chemicals that ease pain and stress. Nobody has proven that yet. But it would explain a lot.

It also fits with the kitten story. A purr was the sound of safety from the very first days of life. So when a grown cat feels scared or sore it falls back on that old sound. It is almost like the cat is telling itself that everything will be okay.

This matters a lot for you as an owner. A purring cat is not always a healthy cat. Cats are very good at hiding pain. In the wild a cat that looks weak can quickly become a target. So a sick cat might hide away and purr. If your cat is purring but also hiding or not eating, please call your vet.

Now here comes the most surprising idea of all. Some scientists think the purr might help a cat heal.

You have probably heard the old saying that cats have nine lives. Cats do seem to bounce back from injuries remarkably well. There are old stories among vets that cats mend broken bones faster than dogs do. Cats also seem to have fewer problems with their bones and joints as they age.

Around the year 2001 a researcher named Elizabeth von Muggenthaler recorded the purrs of many different cats. She recorded house cats and cheetahs and pumas and more. She found that most of the purrs sat in a low range of about twenty five to fifty vibrations per second. And that lined up with something interesting from human medicine.

Doctors have studied gentle vibration as a way to help bones grow stronger. Some of the frequencies used in that work fall in the same low range as a purr. So the idea goes like this. A cat resting for long hours might use its purr like a built in massage machine. The low vibration could keep its bones and muscles strong while it naps. It could even help an injury mend a little faster.

It is a lovely idea. But we need to be honest about it. Nobody has proven that purring heals cats. It is a theory that fits some of the facts. Nobody has done the big careful studies to show that it really works. So treat it as a fascinating maybe and not as a fact.

Some people go one step further. They say that a purring cat can help heal humans too. That has not been proven either. But many cat owners will tell you that a purring cat lowers their stress. And one large study found that cat owners were less likely to die of a heart attack. Whether the purr is the reason is anyone's guess. Still it is hard to argue with a warm cat rumbling on your chest after a bad day.

Now let's talk about the purr your cat uses on you.

Have you ever been woken up at five in the morning by a purring cat? A cat that sits on your pillow and purrs right into your face? There is a reason that purr is so hard to ignore.

In 2009 a scientist named Karen McComb at the University of Sussex looked into this. Her own cat liked to wake her up early for breakfast. So she and her team recorded cats purring in two different situations. In the first the cats were simply relaxed. In the second the cats were asking their owners for food.

Then they played both kinds of purr to people. Even people who had never owned a cat could tell them apart. The food purrs sounded more urgent and less pleasant. People found them much harder to ignore.

When the team looked closer they found the secret. Hidden inside the hungry purr was a high pitched sound. It was a little like a cry mixed into the rumble. And it sat in a range close to the cry of a human baby. Humans are wired to respond to a crying baby. So the cat had found a way to tug on that very same string.

Scientists call it the solicitation purr. It is a purr with a request built right in. Cats that live closely with one person seem to use it the most. They learn over time exactly which sound gets the bowl filled. In other words your cat may have trained you. And it did all of that with a purr.

So listen closely the next time your cat purrs at you near the kitchen. You may hear that little cry tucked inside the rumble. That is not just love talking. That is a breakfast order.

Purrs come in all sorts of sizes too. Some cats purr so softly that you only notice by touching their throat. Others purr so loudly that you can hear them across the room.

The loudest purr on record belonged to a cat named Merlin from Devon in England. In 2015 his purr was measured at almost sixty eight decibels. That is about as loud as two people talking. It is close to the hum of a running dishwasher. All of that came out of one ordinary house cat.

Some cats purr while they eat. Some cats purr in their sleep. Some start purring the moment you look at them. And some cats hardly ever purr at all. That is completely normal too. A quiet cat is not an unhappy cat. Some cats simply show their feelings in other ways.

So how do you know what your cat's purr means? The trick is to look at the whole cat and not just listen.

A happy purr usually comes with a relaxed body. The eyes are half closed or blinking slowly. The tail lies calm and still. The cat may knead or rub its face against you. That is the purr of a cat that feels safe.

A stressed purr looks very different. The body is tense or hunched up. The ears may be pulled back flat. The cat might be hiding or breathing fast. That purr is a cat trying to calm itself down.

And the hungry purr usually comes with a cat that is staring at you. Or staring at the food bowl. Or walking back and forth between the two. You will learn that one fast. Your cat will make sure of that.

So why does your cat purr?

Because it is the first language it ever spoke. It started in a pile of blind kittens beside a warm mother cat. Because the purr is how a cat tells you that it feels safe. Because it is also how a scared or hurt cat calms itself down. Because it might even help its bones and body heal. And because some clever cats have learned to use it to get breakfast.

A purr is not just one message. It is many messages hidden in one rumble. To understand it you have to look at the whole cat.

But here is the part I like best. Your cat once purred only for its mother in those first blind days. Now it purrs for you. So enjoy it the next time that warm little motor starts up on your chest. That sound is the oldest way a cat knows to say that you are family.
