Why does your cat purr? Ask most people and they'll say a purr just means a happy cat. But cats also purr at the vet and when they're badly hurt. So what is that purr really trying to tell you?

Picture a slow Sunday morning and you're still in bed. The cat climbs up and plants itself right on your chest. A few seconds later the little motor kicks in. It's a soft and steady rumble that you feel more than you actually hear. Your whole ribcage seems to hum along with it. The cat's eyes drift shut and somehow the room gets a little quieter.

It might be one of the nicest sounds in the world. It's also one of the strangest. For a very long time nobody could even explain how a cat makes it. And the reasons behind it turn out to be way more interesting than plain old happiness.

So let's go right back to the start. Let's go back to a cardboard box full of newborn kittens.

A kitten comes into the world blind and deaf. Its eyes stay sealed for roughly the first week or two. Its ears are folded down and closed up tight. It can't walk yet and it can't keep itself warm. About all it can manage is to wriggle toward its mother and eat.

Now think about the problem that leaves the mother with. She's got a heap of tiny kittens that can't see her or hear her. How is she supposed to let them know she's close by? And how do they let her know they're doing alright?

The answer turns out to be the purr. Kittens start purring when they're only a few days old. The mother purrs right back at them while they nurse. A deaf kitten can't hear much of anything. What it can do is feel a vibration running through its whole little body. So the purr becomes a quiet signal passed between them. The mother is saying that she's right here. The kittens are saying that everything is fine.

There's a clever little detail hiding in this too. A kitten can't meow while it's drinking milk. Its mouth is far too busy for that. But it can purr with a mouth full of milk just fine. So the purr lets a kitten talk to its mom while it eats.

That means purring is one of the very first things a cat ever does. It belongs to the warmest and safest days of its whole life. And just like kneading it never really goes away.

So when your grown cat purrs on your lap it's speaking its oldest language. A lot of experts think it's treating you a bit like its mother. You're warm and you're soft and you're the one who brings the food. From where the cat is sitting that's a pretty decent mom.

But how does a cat actually make that sound? Honestly this one stumped scientists for a surprisingly long time.

Some of the early guesses were a little out there. One old theory said the purr came from blood rushing through a big vein in the chest. Another claimed cats had a second set of vocal cords just for purring. Neither one held up in the end.

For years the best answer came down to muscles. Tucked inside a cat's throat is the voice box. When a cat purrs the muscles around that voice box twitch incredibly fast. We're talking twenty to thirty twitches every single second. Each twitch snaps the gap between the vocal cords open and shut again. The air rushing through gets chopped up into that quick and steady rumble. And the cat keeps it going while it breathes in and while it breathes out. That's why a purr can roll on and on without a single break.

Most cat sounds only happen on the way out. A meow is made with air going out. A hiss is made with air going out too. The purr is special because it runs in both directions. If you listen really closely you can sometimes catch it shifting as the cat breathes.

Then in 2023 a team of scientists turned up a twist. They studied the voice boxes of cats that had died of illness. And they found a voice box could make a purring sound with no twitching muscles at all. Just blowing air through it was enough to do the trick. Inside the vocal cords they spotted small pads of soft tissue. Those pads make the cords heavier so they can wobble very slowly. That's how such a small animal pulls off such a deep sound.

So the real answer is probably a bit of both. The brain and the muscles get the purr started and keep it going. The odd build of the voice box gives it that deep and steady rumble. Scientists are still sorting out the details. And I find it kind of amazing that one of the most familiar sounds in your house is still partly a mystery.

Here's another fun one for you. Not every cat can purr at all.

Your house cat purrs and so does a cheetah. So does a puma and a lynx and a bobcat. The big famous cats are a different story though. Lions and tigers and leopards and jaguars can't purr the way your cat does. Those four are the ones that can roar.

For a long time people thought it all came down to a small bone in the throat. In the roaring cats part of that bone stays soft and stretchy. That was supposed to explain why they roar instead of purr. These days the picture looks a lot messier. The shape of their vocal cords seems to matter quite a bit too. A lion can make a sort of purring sound as it breathes out. What it can't do is keep the rumble going in and out like your cat.

So there's a rough rule in the cat family. Most cats get to purr or roar but not both. Very few of them can really pull off the two. Your little cat ended up with the purr. And honestly that's the better deal when you're curled up on a lap.

People have been charmed by this sound for ages. The English word purr was first written down back in the 1600s. It was made to sound just like the noise it describes. Plenty of other languages did the exact same thing. In French a contented cat goes ronron. In German a happy cat will schnurren. In Japanese the purr is goro goro. Pretty much anywhere people live with cats they came up with a word for that rumble.

Okay so now we get to the real puzzle. If a purr means a happy cat then why do cats purr when they're miserable?

Ask any vet and you'll hear the same thing. Cats purr up on the exam table. They purr when they're frightened. They purr when they're injured and in real pain. Mother cats purr while they're giving birth. And some cats purr softly when they're very old and close to the end.

None of those are what you'd call happy moments. So the purr can't simply mean a cat is content.

A lot of experts now see the purr as a way for a cat to calm itself down. It's a bit like a person humming under their breath when they're nervous. Or rocking back and forth a little when something hurts. That steady rhythm seems to soothe the cat. Some scientists think purring might even help the body release chemicals that ease pain and stress. Nobody has proven that one yet. But it would explain an awful lot.

It fits the kitten story too. From the very first days of life a purr was the sound of being safe. So when a grown cat feels scared or sore it reaches back for that old sound. It's almost as if the cat is telling itself that it's going to be okay.

And this matters for you as an owner. A purring cat isn't always a healthy cat. Cats are remarkably good at hiding pain. Out in the wild a cat that looks weak can quickly become somebody's dinner. So a sick cat might tuck itself away somewhere and purr. If your cat is purring but also hiding or skipping meals, please give your vet a call.

Now here's what I think is the most surprising idea of the lot. Some scientists think the purr might actually help a cat heal.

You've probably heard the old saying that cats have nine lives. And cats do seem to bounce back from injuries remarkably well. There are old stories among vets that cats mend broken bones faster than dogs do. Cats also seem to run into fewer problems with their bones and joints as they get older.

Around the year 2001 a researcher named Elizabeth von Muggenthaler recorded the purrs of lots of different cats. She recorded house cats and cheetahs and pumas and more besides. Most of those purrs sat in a low range of about twenty five to fifty vibrations per second. And that happened to line up with something interesting from human medicine.

Doctors have studied gentle vibration as a way to help bones grow stronger. Some of the frequencies used in that work land in the same low range as a purr. So the idea goes something like this. A cat lounging around for hours on end might use its purr like a built in massage machine. That low buzz could keep its bones and muscles strong while it naps the day away. It might even help an injury knit back together a bit faster.

It's a lovely idea and I really want it to be true. But we have to be honest about where it stands. Nobody has actually proven that purring heals cats. It's a theory that happens to fit some of the facts. Nobody has done the big careful studies that would show it really works. So file it under fascinating maybe and not under settled fact.

Some people take it one step further. They say a purring cat can help heal humans as well. That hasn't been proven either. Still plenty of cat owners will tell you a purring cat takes the edge off their stress. And one large study found that cat owners were less likely to die of a heart attack. Whether the purr is the reason is anybody's guess. But it's hard to argue with a warm cat rumbling on your chest after a rotten day.

Now let's talk about the purr your cat saves just for you.

Have you ever been woken up at five in the morning by a purring cat? One that parks itself on your pillow and purrs straight into your face? There's a reason that particular purr is so hard to ignore.

In 2009 a scientist named Karen McComb at the University of Sussex decided to dig into this. Her own cat had a habit of waking her up early for breakfast. So she and her team recorded cats purring in two different situations. In the first one the cats were just relaxing. In the second one the cats were asking their owners for food.

Then they played both kinds of purr back to people. Even folks who had never owned a cat could tell them apart. The food purrs came across as more urgent and less pleasant. People found them a whole lot harder to tune out.

When the team dug a little deeper they found the secret. Hidden inside the hungry purr was a high pitched sound. It was a bit like a cry stirred into the rumble. And it sat in a range close to the cry of a human baby. People are wired to respond when a baby cries. So the cat had figured out how to tug on that very same string.

Scientists call this the solicitation purr. Basically it's a purr with a request baked right in. Cats that live closely with one person seem to use it the most. Over time they learn exactly which sound gets the bowl filled. In other words your cat may well have trained you. And it pulled the whole thing off with a purr.

So listen closely the next time your cat purrs at you near the kitchen. You might catch that little cry tucked inside the rumble. That isn't just love doing the talking. That's a breakfast order being placed.

Purrs come in all sorts of sizes too. Some cats purr so softly you only notice when your fingers brush their throat. Others purr so loudly you can hear them from across the room.

The loudest purr on record belonged to a cat named Merlin from Devon in England. Back in 2015 his purr was measured at almost sixty eight decibels. That's about as loud as two people chatting. It's close to the hum of a dishwasher running in the kitchen. And all of that came out of one ordinary house cat.

Some cats purr while they eat. Some cats purr right through their naps. Some start up the moment you so much as glance at them. And some cats hardly ever purr at all. That's completely normal as well. A quiet cat isn't an unhappy cat. Some cats just show how they feel in other ways.

So how can you tell what your cat's purr means? The trick is to watch the whole cat instead of only listening.

A happy purr usually comes with a loose and relaxed body. The eyes are half closed or doing that slow blink. The tail lies calm and still. The cat might knead or rub its face against you. That's the purr of a cat that feels completely safe.

A stressed purr looks pretty different. The body is tense or hunched up tight. The ears may be pulled back flat. The cat might be hiding or breathing a little fast. That purr is a cat working hard to calm itself down.

And the hungry purr tends to come with a cat that is staring right at you. Or staring at the food bowl instead. Or pacing back and forth between the two of you. You'll learn that one fast enough. Trust me your cat will make sure of it.

So why does your cat purr?

Because it's the very first language it ever spoke. It started in a pile of blind kittens snuggled against a warm mother. Because the purr is how a cat tells you that it feels safe. Because it's also how a scared or hurting cat calms itself down. Because it might even help its bones and body heal. And because some clever cats have learned to use it to get breakfast.

A purr isn't just one message. It's a whole bundle of messages wrapped up in one rumble. To read it properly you have to look at the whole cat.

But here's the part I like best. Your cat once purred only for its mother in those first blind days. Now it purrs for you. So soak it in the next time that warm little motor starts up on your chest. That sound is the oldest way a cat knows to tell you that you're family.
