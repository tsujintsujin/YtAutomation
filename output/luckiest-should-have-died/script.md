# The Luckiest People Who Should Have Died

## Vesna Vulović

Vesna Vulović was a flight attendant for Yugoslavia's national airline. On January twenty-sixth, nineteen seventy-two, she went to work on a flight she was never supposed to be on. The airline had mixed her up with another flight attendant. Same first name.

High above the clouds, the plane blew up. Investigators later said it was a bomb. It broke into three pieces, more than ten kilometres up. That's higher than Mount Everest.

A fall like that should kill anyone. And it killed everyone else on board.

But Vesna was way at the back, in the tail. A food cart had her pinned against the wall in there. So she never fell out. She rode the tail all the way down.

The tail hit thick snow. At just the right angle.

She broke her skull. Both legs. Three bones in her back, and her hips. She was in a coma for days, and when she woke up, she remembered nothing at all. To this day, it's the highest fall anyone has ever survived without a parachute.

And once she was back on her feet? She went back to work. For the same airline.

## Michael Packard

Michael Packard was a fifty-six-year-old lobster diver in Cape Cod, in the United States. June eleventh, two thousand twenty-one, was just another workday. Down he went, looking for lobsters.

Then something hit him. Everything went black.

His first thought was a shark. But there were no teeth. No teeth at all.

And then it clicked. He was inside the mouth of a humpback whale.

He still had his air, so he kept breathing. And he thought about his two boys at home, twelve and fifteen.

So why is he still here? A humpback's mouth is huge, sure. But it has no teeth. And its throat is far too small to swallow a person.

After about thirty seconds, the whale came up to the surface, shook its head, and spat him out into the sea. Like a seed.

His crewmate pulled him into the boat. Bruises all over. Not one broken bone. He was out of hospital the very same day.

## Phineas Gage

Phineas Gage ran a railway crew in Vermont. His men blasted rock out of the way with gunpowder. Everyday work.

The way it went: drill a hole in the rock, pour in the gunpowder, then press it down with a long iron rod.

On September thirteenth, eighteen forty-eight, the rod scraped the rock. A spark. The gunpowder went off, and it fired that rod like a bullet.

It went in under his left cheekbone, straight through his brain, and out the top of his head. Then it landed twenty-five metres behind him.

Phineas went down. And a few minutes later, he was awake. And talking.

He rode into town sitting up in an ox cart, of all things.

He lived for almost twelve more years. But his friends said something about him had changed. His doctor wrote that, to them, he was "no longer Gage."

## Harrison Okene

Harrison Okene was a twenty-nine-year-old cook on a tugboat, working near an oil platform off the coast of Nigeria.

At about ten to five in the morning, on May twenty-sixth, two thousand thirteen, rough waves flipped the boat over. It sank upside down, thirty metres to the bottom of the sea. Picture a ten-storey building. That deep.

Harrison was in the toilet when it happened. Water rushed in, and everything went dark. There were eleven other men on board. Not one of them made it.

But Harrison found a pocket of air, trapped inside the upside-down boat. Just over a metre high. So he stood there in the dark, with his head in that little bubble.

He had one can of cola. His two torches died within a day. And he waited.

About sixty hours later, divers showed up. They'd been sent to bring up bodies. One of them swam right past him. So Harrison reached out, and grabbed his hand.

Out of twelve men, he was the only one who survived. But he couldn't just swim up. After that long, that deep, coming up too fast could have killed him. So first, he spent another two and a half days in a pressure chamber.

## Ann Hodges

Ann Hodges lived in a small town in Alabama. On November thirtieth, nineteen fifty-four, she lay down on her couch for an afternoon nap.

Then a rock, about four kilos, smashed through her roof. A rock from space.

It bounced off her big wooden radio, and slammed into her hip.

She woke up with a huge bruise. And just like that, she was the first person we know of who was ever hit, and hurt, by a meteorite.

Pretty soon, reporters were crowding around her house. Everybody wanted to see the space rock.

And then the rock got her into trouble. Her landlady said it was hers, since it fell on her house. So she took Ann to court.

In the end, they made a deal, and the landlady gave up her claim. Two years later, Ann gave the rock to a museum in Alabama. It's still on show there today.

## Betty Lou Oliver

Betty Lou Oliver was twenty, and she ran an elevator in the Empire State Building in New York. On the morning of July twenty-eighth, nineteen forty-five, a thick fog covered the city.

And out of that fog, an army bomber plane flew straight into the building.

The crash threw Betty out of her elevator. She was badly burned. People gave her first aid, then put her in another elevator to get her down.

What nobody knew? The crash had cut that elevator's cables. The doors closed. And the elevator dropped. Seventy-five floors. All the way to the very bottom.

A fall like that should kill anyone. But two crazy things saved her.

First, the cut cables had already fallen down the shaft. They piled up at the bottom like a giant spring, and the elevator landed on top of them. Second, the air trapped under the falling elevator pushed back up, and slowed it down.

She broke her hips, her back and her neck. But she lived. It's still the longest elevator fall anyone has ever survived.

Five months later, she went back. And rode an elevator again.

## Roy Sullivan

Roy Sullivan was a park ranger in the mountains of Virginia. Over thirty-five years, he was struck by lightning seven times. Seven. And he survived every single one.

The first time was in nineteen forty-two, up in a fire lookout tower. It burned a hole right through his shoe.

Then, for twenty-seven years, nothing. Until it found him again, in his truck. Knocked him out, and burned off his eyebrows.

The next year it got him in his own front yard, and burned his shoulder.

Two years after that, it hit him inside a ranger station and set his hair on fire. The year after, his hair caught fire again. So he poured a can of water over his own head.

Then it got his ankle.

The seventh time, in nineteen seventy-seven, he was out fishing. The bolt hit him right on top of the head. And then, as if that wasn't enough, a bear showed up and tried to steal his fish. So Roy hit the bear with a branch.

He's in the world record book as the person hit by lightning more times than anyone else.

## Juliane Koepcke

Juliane Koepcke was seventeen, flying over the rainforest in Peru with her mother. It was Christmas Eve, nineteen seventy-one.

The plane flew into a storm. Lightning hit it, and it broke apart in the air.

Juliane fell three kilometres. Still strapped into her row of seats.

She woke up on the jungle floor. Alone. Her collarbone was broken. She had a deep cut on her arm, a hurt eye and a hurt knee. And there was nobody around.

So she found a small creek, and she followed it. Day after day, walking and wading downstream, until the creek turned into a river.

After nine days, she found an empty camp by the river. It belonged to lumberjacks. When they came back, there she was.

She'd been in the jungle for eleven days. Out of everyone on that plane, she was the only one who made it out.

And here's the thing. She went back. She grew up to study bats, and today she runs a research station in that same rainforest in Peru.

## Bill Morgan

Bill Morgan was a truck driver in Australia. In nineteen ninety-eight, after a car crash, doctors gave him a medicine. One he was allergic to.

His heart stopped. For fourteen minutes, he was dead.

The doctors got his heart going again. But he slipped into a coma, and it didn't look good. Doctors told his family to think about turning off his machines.

Then, after twelve days, Bill woke up.

About a year later, he bought a scratch card. And won a car.

That story was so good that a TV news crew came out to film it. They asked him to buy one more scratch card, just for the cameras, to show how it happened.

So Bill bought one more card. And scratched it right there, on camera.

Two hundred fifty thousand dollars.

## José Salvador Alvarenga

José Salvador Alvarenga was a fisherman from El Salvador, working in Mexico. On November seventeenth, two thousand twelve, he went out fishing with a young crewmate named Ezequiel.

A storm hit. It broke their motor and blew them far out into the Pacific Ocean. Nobody was coming for them.

So José learned to live on that boat. He caught fish, turtles and birds with his bare hands. He drank rainwater. And when there was no rain? Turtle blood.

But after a while, Ezequiel died. Now José was alone.

He kept drifting. For months. Across the biggest ocean on Earth.

Then one day, he saw a tiny island. He jumped into the water, swam for it, and made it to the beach.

He'd been at sea for four hundred thirty-eight days. And he'd drifted about ten thousand kilometres, from Mexico all the way to the Marshall Islands.

## Violet Jessop

Violet Jessop worked on giant ocean liners, looking after the passengers. She was on board three ships when disaster hit. And she walked away from all three.

In nineteen eleven, she was on a ship called the Olympic when it crashed into a navy warship. Violet was fine.

The next year, she joined the Olympic's sister ship. The Titanic. When it sank, Violet was ordered into a lifeboat, and a ship's officer handed her a baby to look after.

Four years later, during the war, she was a nurse on the third sister ship, the Britannic. It hit a mine and started to sink.

Violet got into a lifeboat. But the ship's giant propellers were still turning, and they were ripping the lifeboats apart. So she jumped out of hers, into the sea. She hit her head hard. But she lived.

And after all of that? She went back to sea. She worked on ships for another thirty years.

## Tsutomu Yamaguchi

Tsutomu Yamaguchi was an engineer for a big Japanese company. In August, nineteen forty-five, they sent him to Hiroshima for work.

On the morning of August sixth, he was walking to the docks, ready to go home. At a quarter past eight, the first atomic bomb went off.

The blast burned the left side of his body and burst his eardrums. He spent the night in the ruins of the city.

Then he went home to Nagasaki.

On August ninth, still covered in bandages, he went back to work. He was in the office, telling his boss about the giant flash in Hiroshima. And right then, he said, the same white light filled the room.

A second atomic bomb had hit his home city.

Somehow, he survived that one too. Years later, Japan officially recognised him as a survivor of both bombs.

He lived to be ninety-three.
