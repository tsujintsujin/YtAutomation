# The Luckiest People Who Should Have Died

## Vesna Vulović

Vesna Vulović was a flight attendant for Yugoslavia's national airline. On January twenty-sixth, nineteen seventy-two, she was working on a flight. She was never supposed to be on it. The airline had mixed her up with another flight attendant who had the same first name.

High above the clouds, the plane exploded. Investigators later said it was a bomb. The plane broke into three pieces, more than ten kilometres up. That's higher than Mount Everest.

A fall like that should kill anyone. And everyone else on board died.

But Vesna was at the very back, in the tail. And a food cart had her pinned against the wall in there. So she stayed inside the tail, all the way down.

Then the tail came down in thick snow. And it hit at just the right angle.

She broke her skull, both legs, three bones in her back, and her hips. She was in a coma for days, and she remembered nothing. To this day, it's the highest fall anyone has ever survived without a parachute.

And once she could walk again, she went back to work. For the same airline.

## Michael Packard

Michael Packard was a fifty-six-year-old lobster diver in Cape Cod, in the United States. On June eleventh, two thousand twenty-one, he went diving for lobsters, like on any other workday.

Under the water, something hit him. And everything went black. His first thought was a shark. But he couldn't feel any teeth.

Then he understood where he was. He was inside the mouth of a humpback whale.

He still had his air, so he kept breathing. And he thought about his two boys, twelve and fifteen, at home.

Here's why he lived. A humpback's mouth is huge. But it has no teeth at all. And its throat is far too small to swallow a person.

After about thirty seconds, the whale rose to the surface, shook its head, and spat him out into the sea.

His crewmate pulled him into the boat. He had bruises all over, but not one broken bone. He was out of hospital that same day.

## Phineas Gage

Phineas Gage was the foreman of a railway crew in Vermont. His men blasted rock out of the way with gunpowder. It was everyday work.

First, you drill a hole in the rock. Then you pour in the gunpowder. Then you press it down with a long iron rod.

On September thirteenth, eighteen forty-eight, the rod scraped the rock and made a spark. The gunpowder went off, and it fired the rod out like a bullet.

It went in under his left cheekbone, straight through his brain, and out the top of his head. Then it landed twenty-five metres behind him.

Phineas fell to the ground. And a few minutes later, he was awake. And talking.

He rode into town sitting up in an ox cart.

Phineas lived for almost twelve more years. But his friends said something about him had changed. His doctor wrote that, to them, he was "no longer Gage."

## Harrison Okene

Harrison Okene was a twenty-nine-year-old cook on a tugboat, working near an oil platform off the coast of Nigeria.

At about ten to five in the morning, on May twenty-sixth, two thousand thirteen, rough waves flipped the boat over. It sank upside down, thirty metres to the bottom of the sea. That's about as deep as a ten-storey building is tall.

Harrison was in the toilet when it happened. Water poured in, and everything went dark. Eleven other men were on board. None of them made it.

But Harrison found a small pocket of air, trapped in the upside-down boat. It was just over a metre high. He stood in the dark with his head in that bubble.

He had one can of cola. His two torches died within a day. And he waited.

After about sixty hours, divers came. They had been sent to bring up bodies. One of them swam right past him. So Harrison reached out and grabbed his hand.

He was the only one of twelve men to survive. But he couldn't just swim up. After so long that deep, coming up too fast could have killed him. So he spent another two and a half days in a pressure chamber first.

## Ann Hodges

Ann Hodges lived in a small town in Alabama. On November thirtieth, nineteen fifty-four, she lay down on her couch for an afternoon nap.

Then a rock, about four kilos, crashed through her roof. It came straight from space.

It bounced off her big wooden radio, and slammed into her hip.

Ann woke up with a huge bruise on her hip. And she became the first person we know of who was ever hit and hurt by a meteorite.

Soon, reporters were crowding around her house. Everyone wanted to see the rock from space.

Then the rock got her into trouble. Her landlady said it was hers, because it had fallen on her house. So the landlady took her to court.

In the end, they made a deal, and Ann got her rock back. And two years later, she gave it to a museum in Alabama. It's still on show there today.

## Betty Lou Oliver

Betty Lou Oliver was a twenty-year-old elevator operator in the Empire State Building in New York. On the morning of July twenty-eighth, nineteen forty-five, a thick fog covered the city.

In that fog, an army bomber plane flew straight into the building.

The crash threw Betty out of her elevator. She was badly burned. People gave her first aid, and then put her in another elevator to take her down.

But nobody knew the crash had cut that elevator's cables. The doors closed. And the elevator dropped. Seventy-five floors, all the way to the very bottom.

A fall like that should kill anyone. But two crazy things saved her.

First, the cut cables had fallen down the shaft before her. They piled up at the bottom like a giant spring, and the elevator landed on them. Second, the air trapped under the falling elevator pushed back, and slowed it down.

She broke her hips, her back and her neck. But she lived. It's still the longest elevator fall anyone has survived.

And five months later, she went back. And rode an elevator again.

## Roy Sullivan

Roy Sullivan was a park ranger in the mountains of Virginia. Over thirty-five years, he was struck by lightning seven times. And he survived every single one.

The first time was in nineteen forty-two, in a fire lookout tower. The lightning burned a hole right through his shoe.

Then nothing happened for twenty-seven years. Until it hit him in his truck. It knocked him out, and burned off his eyebrows.

The next year, it got him in his own front yard, and burned his shoulder.

Two years later, it hit him inside a ranger station, and set his hair on fire. The year after that, his hair caught fire again. So he poured a can of water over his own head.

Then it got his ankle.

The seventh time, in nineteen seventy-seven, he was out fishing. The bolt hit him right on top of the head. And then a bear came and tried to steal his fish. So Roy hit the bear with a branch.

He's in the world record book as the person hit by lightning more times than anyone else.

## Juliane Koepcke

Juliane Koepcke was a seventeen-year-old girl, flying over the rainforest in Peru with her mother. It was Christmas Eve, nineteen seventy-one.

The plane flew into a storm. Lightning hit it, and it broke apart in the air.

Juliane fell three kilometres, still strapped into her row of seats.

She woke up on the jungle floor, alone. Her collarbone was broken. She had a deep cut on her arm, a hurt eye and a hurt knee. And she couldn't find anyone.

So she found a small creek, and she followed it. Day after day, she walked and waded downstream, until it became a river.

After nine days, she found an empty camp by the river. It belonged to lumberjacks. When they came back, they found her there.

She had been in the jungle for eleven days. Out of everyone on that plane, she was the only one who made it out.

And she went back. She grew up to study bats, and today she runs a research station in that same rainforest in Peru.

## Bill Morgan

Bill Morgan was a truck driver in Australia. In nineteen ninety-eight, after a car crash, doctors gave him a medicine. But he was allergic to it.

His heart stopped. For fourteen minutes, he was dead.

Then the doctors got his heart beating again. But he fell into a coma. And it didn't look good. Doctors told his family to think about turning off his machines.

Then, after twelve days, Bill woke up.

About a year later, he bought a scratch card. And he won a car.

His story was so good that a TV news crew came to film it. They asked him to buy another scratch card, just for the cameras, to show how it happened.

So Bill bought one more card, and scratched it right there, on camera.

And he won two hundred fifty thousand dollars.

## José Salvador Alvarenga

José Salvador Alvarenga was a fisherman from El Salvador, working in Mexico. On November seventeenth, two thousand twelve, he went out fishing with a young crewmate, Ezequiel.

A storm hit. It broke their motor, and blew them far out into the Pacific Ocean. Nobody was coming.

So José learned to live on the boat. He caught fish, turtles and birds with his bare hands. He drank rainwater. When there was no rain, he drank turtle blood.

But after a while, Ezequiel died. José was alone.

He kept drifting. For months. Across the biggest ocean on Earth.

Then, one day, he saw a tiny island. He jumped into the water and swam for it, and made it to the beach.

He had been at sea for four hundred thirty-eight days. And he had drifted about ten thousand kilometres, from Mexico all the way to the Marshall Islands.

## Violet Jessop

Violet Jessop worked on giant ocean liners, looking after the passengers. She was on board three ships when disaster hit. And she walked away from all three.

In nineteen eleven, she was on a ship called the Olympic, when it crashed into a navy warship. Violet was fine.

The next year, she joined the Olympic's sister ship. The Titanic. When it sank, Violet was ordered into a lifeboat. And a ship's officer handed her a baby to look after.

Four years later, during the war, she was a nurse on the third sister ship, the Britannic. It hit a mine and began to sink.

Violet got into a lifeboat. But the ship's giant propellers were still turning, and they were ripping the lifeboats apart. So she jumped out of hers, into the sea. She hit her head hard. But she lived.

And after all of that, she went back to sea. She worked on ships for another thirty years.

## Tsutomu Yamaguchi

Tsutomu Yamaguchi was an engineer for a big Japanese company. In August, nineteen forty-five, his company sent him to Hiroshima for work.

On the morning of August sixth, he was walking to the docks, about to go home. At a quarter past eight, the first atomic bomb went off.

The blast burned the left side of his body, and burst his eardrums. He spent the night in the ruins of the city.

Then he went home to Nagasaki.

On August ninth, covered in bandages, he went back to work. He was in the office, telling his boss about the giant flash in Hiroshima. And right then, he said, the same white light filled the room.

A second atomic bomb had hit his home city.

Somehow, he survived that one too. Years later, Japan officially recognised him as a survivor of both bombs.

He lived to be ninety-three years old.
