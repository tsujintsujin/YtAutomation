## Channel description

Why does your cat shove the pen off the desk while staring right at you? Why does a dog turn three circles before it finally flops down? Pets do a lot of weird stuff, and most of it has a reason. Sometimes a really old one.

That's what this channel is about. Pet behavior, the why behind it, and the history of how our animals ended up doing it in the first place. One habit per video, followed all the way back: where it started, what the actual research says, and what it means for the little creature asleep on your couch right now.

You'll see a lot of a Geek and his silver tabby. The cat follows him into the bathroom. He's made his peace with it, mostly.

If you've ever looked at your pet and just thought "...why?", you're in the right place.

## Video 3

### Title
Why Does Your Cat Purr?

### Description
Sunday morning. You're still in bed, half awake, and the cat climbs up and parks itself right on your chest. Give it a few seconds and the little motor kicks in. Everybody "knows" a purr means a happy cat... except cats purr at the vet too. They purr when they're hurt. Some even purr while giving birth. So what is that rumble actually telling you?

It goes all the way back to a pile of newborn kittens, blind and deaf, who can't hear their mother but can definitely feel her purr (and who, handily, can purr with a mouth full of milk). From there we get into how a cat even makes the sound: an old theory about blood rushing through a vein, the twitching muscles in the voice box, and a 2023 study where a voice box purred with no muscles involved at all. Also why lions and tigers can roar but can't purr, ronron and goro goro, why scared or sick cats purr to calm themselves down, and the idea that purr frequencies might help bones heal. Interesting, but not proven, so take that one with a pinch of salt. Then there's the 2009 Sussex study that found a hidden cry tucked inside the "feed me" purr, one that tugs at us the same way a baby's cry does. Plus Merlin, the loudest purring cat on record, and how to read a purr by watching the whole cat, not just listening.

### Title
Why Does Your Cat Knock Things Off the Table?

### Description
You make a cup of tea, set it down and turn away for one second. One. When you look back the cat's sitting right beside it, staring at you, a single paw resting on the mug. And it starts to slide. Slowly. Toward the edge. Eye contact the entire time... and then it's gone. Pens, keys, earrings, the odd houseplant, it all ends up on the floor sooner or later. So is your cat a tiny furry villain? Or is there something else going on in there?

Honestly it goes back to a small desert wildcat that had to hunt a lot of little meals every day, and that learned to give anything that might be playing dead a good poke. From there we get into kittens practicing the swat, why cats see pretty badly up close (so the paws and whiskers do the looking), a 2016 Kyoto University study hinting that cats get the idea that things fall, and the noise, which might be half the fun. Then the stare. That part's really about you, because to a cat any attention counts as attention, even the annoyed kind. Also the breakfast alarm, clearing your desk while you're trying to work, plain boredom, young cats versus old ones, and why cats love being up high in the first place. And what to actually do about it: don't punish, put the breakables away, play every day (ideally right before meals), puzzle feeders, a knocking shelf of its very own, and when a sudden change is worth a word with your vet.

### Title
Why Do Cats Love Boxes So Much?

### Description
A parcel turns up. You pull out the fancy new cat bed you ordered, set it down all nice... and the cat walks right past it and climbs into the box. And the size of the box? Doesn't matter in the slightest. A big chunky cat will happily cram itself into a shoe box with half of it hanging over the sides. So what is it about cardboard, exactly?

It starts with a small desert wildcat that was a hunter and, at the same time, somebody else's dinner. For an animal like that a tight hiding spot meant two things: safety, and a good place to ambush from. From there we get into a 2014 Utrecht University study where shelter cats that were given boxes settled in faster and were less stressed, why your cat ends up in drawers, sinks and shopping bags, mother cats hunting for a dark nest, and why the open, fancy bed loses every time. Then warmth (cats like it a lot warmer than we do, and cardboard holds the heat), boxes as something new to explore and claim with the scent glands on their cheeks, and honestly the strangest part: the square of tape on the floor, plus a 2021 study by Gabriella Smith where cats sat in a Kanizsa illusion square about as often as a real one. Also lions and tigers going wild over giant boxes, how to give your cat a box safely, and when a sudden change in hiding is worth a trip to the vet.
