# A metal detecting lesson found £4.3 million

A couple took five friends out to teach them metal detecting. That day, they found coins worth over four million pounds. But first, two dads and their sons who went detecting together.

In two thousand seven, a father and son were metal detecting in a field near Harrogate, in England. They dug up an old silver cup. Packed inside were more than six hundred silver coins, plus silver jewellery and a gold arm ring. It had been hidden in Viking times, over a thousand years ago. Two museums bought it for over one million pounds.

Then in twenty ten, a young man and his dad were searching a farm in Cumbria. He dug up dozens of broken bits of bronze. Put back together, they made a Roman rider's helmet, with a full face mask. It was about eighteen hundred years old. Experts guessed it might sell for three hundred thousand pounds. At the auction, it sold for two point three million.

Now, the lesson. In twenty nineteen, Lisa Grace and Adam Staples took five friends to a farm field near Bath. They were showing them how to use metal detectors. Then the beeping started. Silver coins were all over the place. More than two thousand five hundred of them, from right after the year ten sixty-six, when England got a new king. Lots of them showed King Harold. Lots showed King William, the man who beat him. A museum trust bought them for four point three million pounds. Half went to the landowner. The other half was split between all seven friends.

Who would you take treasure hunting?

## Why it is built this way

- One theme: people detecting together (a father and son, a son and his dad, a couple teaching five friends).
- Hook teases the biggest find (the lesson); smallest to biggest: £1.08 million, £2.3 million, £4.3 million.
- "Hidden in Viking times": the script never says who buried it.
- The helmet's estimate (up to £300,000) is the twist: it sold for almost eight times that.
- Lisa Grace and Adam Staples are named: they spoke to the press. The other finders are not named.
- No king is drawn as a likeness: the kings are simple stick figures with crowns on the coins.
