# A £10 ring turned out to be a real diamond

A woman wore a ten-pound ring for years. She thought the stone was fake. It sold for over six hundred fifty thousand pounds. But first, two tiny things that were hiding in plain sight.

In Maine, a man bought an old photo album at a yard sale. He also got some old soda bottles and a couple of chairs. All of it cost less than a hundred dollars. Inside the album was a small photo of a baseball team from eighteen sixty-five, glued onto a card. It was one of the oldest baseball cards ever found. A card just like it sits in the Library of Congress. In twenty thirteen, the card sold for ninety-two thousand dollars.

Then, in nineteen forty-seven, a sixteen-year-old boy in Massachusetts bought lunch at school. In his change was a copper penny from nineteen forty-three. That year, pennies were made of steel, to save copper for the war. Only a few copper ones were made, by mistake. He wrote to the government, and they told him copper ones didn't exist. He kept it anyway, his whole life. After he died, it sold for two hundred four thousand dollars. He left the money to his town's library.

Now, the ring. In the late nineteen eighties, a woman in London paid ten pounds for a box of old jewellery at a car boot sale. One ring had a big, clear stone. She thought it was fake, so she just wore it all the time. About thirty years later, a jeweller took a closer look. It was a real diamond, over twenty-six carats. In twenty seventeen, the ring sold for over six hundred fifty thousand pounds.

What are you wearing right now?

## Why it is built this way

- One theme: small things in plain sight (a photo in an album, a penny in lunch change, a ring on a finger).
- Hook teases the biggest find (the ring); smallest to biggest: $92,000, $204,000, £656,750.
- "One of the oldest baseball cards ever found": the Library of Congress calls its card of the same team and year the first dated baseball card. Reports differ on whether the two are copies of one photo, so the script says "just like it".
- No real people are named or drawn: the card finder, the boy (Don Lutes Jr.) and the ring owner are plain stick figures.
- "Car boot sale" is said as it is: like a yard sale, but out of car boots; the picture shows it.
