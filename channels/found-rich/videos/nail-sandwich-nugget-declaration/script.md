# A $4 painting was hiding $8 million

A four-dollar painting was hiding eight million dollars. But first, three other weird things that made people rich.

A stage worker in Dublin was cleaning up after a Lady Gaga show. On the floor he spotted something small and shiny. He thought it was a guitar pick. It was one of her fake nails. He sold it online for about twelve thousand dollars.

A woman in Miami did even better with her lunch. She made a grilled cheese sandwich, took one bite, and saw a face in the toast. To her, it looked like the Virgin Mary. So she kept it. For ten years. In 2004, an online casino paid twenty-eight thousand dollars for it.

Then came a single chicken nugget. In 2021, someone got a McDonald's nugget shaped like a crewmate from the game Among Us. They put it on eBay for ninety-nine cents. A hundred and eighty-four bids later, it sold for ninety-nine thousand, nine hundred and ninety-seven dollars.

Now, the painting. A man bought it at a flea market in Pennsylvania for four dollars. He only wanted the frame. When he took it apart, he found a folded paper hidden behind the canvas. It was an original copy of the Declaration of Independence, printed in 1776. Only about two dozen are known. In 1991 it sold for two point four million dollars. Nine years later it sold again, for eight million.

What's the weirdest thing you've ever found?

## Why it is built this way

- Fast pace: most lines are cut in two, each half with its own picture, so the picture changes every 1-2 seconds.
- The hook is two pictures: "A four-dollar painting" shows only the boring painting; "was hiding eight million dollars" shows what it hid. Never "this": no real picture is shown, so it is always "a".
- An open loop: the painting is teased first, and what it hid is only said at the end. Picture 29 calls back to picture 1.
- Short lines (under about 2 seconds) stay one picture, so nothing flashes by too fast to read.
- The last line asks the viewer a question, so they answer in the comments.

## Lines and pictures
