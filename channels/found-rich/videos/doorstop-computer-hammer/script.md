# A lost hammer led to £1.75 million

A farmer lost his hammer in a field. Looking for it made two friends rich. But first, two other ordinary things that weren't ordinary at all.

A man in Michigan used a heavy rock to hold a door open. He'd had it for about thirty years. The farmer who sold him the land said it fell from the sky back in the nineteen thirties. In twenty eighteen, he finally took it to a university. It was a real meteorite, a rock from space. It was valued at about a hundred thousand dollars.

Then a woman in California cleared out her garage and dropped some old electronics at a recycling center. She didn't leave her name. Inside one box was an Apple One, one of the very first Apple computers. Only about fifty are left. The recycling company sold it for two hundred thousand dollars, and they wanted to give her half.

Now, the hammer. In nineteen ninety-two, a farmer in England lost his hammer in a field. He asked his friend to find it with a metal detector. First, his friend found silver spoons and gold jewellery. Then thousands of Roman coins. It was the biggest pile of Roman gold and silver ever found in Britain. The finder got one point seven five million pounds, and he shared it with the farmer. And yes, they found the hammer too.

What's the luckiest thing you've ever lost?

## Why it is built this way

- One theme: ordinary things (a doorstop, a box of old electronics, a lost hammer) that hid a fortune.
- Hook teases the biggest story (the hammer) without saying what was found; smallest to biggest: $100,000, $200,000, £1.75 million.
- The meteorite is said as "valued at", not "sold for": no sale was reported.
- "Meteorite" is said in plain words right after it ("a rock from space").
- The last story ends on a small joke (they found the hammer too), then the question.
