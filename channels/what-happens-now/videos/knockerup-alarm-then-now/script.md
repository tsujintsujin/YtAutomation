# Your alarm clock used to be a person

In nineteen hundred, your alarm clock was a person. And they tapped on your bedroom window with a long stick.

Factory workers had to be on time. But clocks were expensive, and not always right. So towns had knocker-uppers. You paid one a few pennies a week. They'd walk the dark streets before dawn. At your house, they lifted a long bamboo pole up to your window. Tap, tap, tap. Some wouldn't leave until they saw you at the window. In London, one woman didn't use a stick. She shot dried peas at the glass, through a long tube. In one mining town, miners chalked their shift times on a slate on the wall, so the knocker-upper knew when to come.

Today, your alarm lives in your phone. Inside is a tiny crystal. Electricity makes it shake, more than thirty-two thousand times a second. A chip counts every shake. That adds up to seconds, then minutes, then hours. Your phone checks the time with the internet, so it stays right. While you sleep, the phone rests, but that counter keeps going. When it hits your time, it wakes the phone, and the alarm goes off. And if you hit snooze, it tries again in a few minutes.

Back then, a stranger tapping on your glass. Now? A crystal in your pocket.

## Why it is built this way

- Template (RULES.md): hook on the surprise (your alarm clock was a person), why the job existed, the knocker-upper
  step by step with "you", the pea shooter and the chalk slate as the odd details, the now step by step (how a phone
  keeps time and wakes you), one closing line.
- Our own angle: the reference Short only names the job; ours walks through the round, the pay, the pea shooter,
  the miners' slates, and what is inside your phone.
- The pea-shooting woman is not named (Mary Smith, East London, 1931): a stick figure, never her likeness.
