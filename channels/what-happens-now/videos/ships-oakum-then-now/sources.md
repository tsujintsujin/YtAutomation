# Sources

- Oakum: old tarry ropes unravelled ("picked") into fibre, work given in prisons and workhouses; 1862 quotas, e.g. men at Coldbath Fields picked 2 lb a day; forced into seams with a hammer and a caulking iron, then sealed with hot pitch: [Wikipedia: Oakum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oakum)
- Caulking wooden ships: fibre driven into the wedge-shaped seam between planks with a caulking mallet and a broad chisel-like caulking iron; seams then paid with melted pine pitch; the workers were caulkers: [Wikipedia: Caulking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caulking)
- Today: most big ships are steel; plates welded into blocks and joined, making a watertight welded skin; blocks pressure washed and grit-blasted, then painted in many coats against corrosion; the underwater hull gets anti-fouling paint so marine growth can't settle: [BAE Systems: Hunter's blast and paint process](https://www.baesystems.com/en-aus/insight/painting-a-picture-of-hunters-blast-and-paint-process), [ILO Encyclopaedia: Ship and boat construction](https://iloencyclopaedia.org/part-xv-26011/ship-and-boat-building-and-repair/item/481-ship-and-boat-construction-and-repair), [Protection of the underwater hull with antifouling coatings](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/PMST240041)
- Taken from (structure only): "How They Used To Make Ships Waterproof" (Zack D. Films). Our own angle (where the oakum came from, today's steel ships), our own wording.
