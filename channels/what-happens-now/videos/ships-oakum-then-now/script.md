# Old rope kept ships from sinking

A wooden ship had a gap between every plank. So they stuffed every gap with old rope.

Back in the eighteen hundreds, the rope came from prisons. Prisoners got piles of old tarry ship rope. They picked it apart with their fingers, strand by strand. Some had to pick two pounds of it a day. The loose fibers were called oakum.

At the shipyard, workers called caulkers rolled the oakum into long strings. They pushed one into a gap between two planks. Then they hammered it in deep, with a mallet and a flat iron chisel. Gap after gap, all over the ship. Next, a pot of black pitch bubbled over a fire. They poured the hot pitch over each stuffed gap. As it cooled, it set hard and sealed the gap shut. Only then could the ship go out to sea.

Today, most big ships are made of steel. Huge steel plates are welded together, so there are no gaps at all. Workers blast the steel clean with tiny bits of grit. Then the hull is sprayed with paint, coat after coat, to stop rust. The bottom gets a special paint, so sea plants and shells can't grow on it. Then it's ready for the ocean.

Back then, old rope and hot pitch. Now? Not one gap to fill.

## Why it is built this way

- Template (RULES.md): hook on the strange fact (old rope in every gap), the before step by step, the now step by
  step, one closing line with both side by side.
- Our own angle: the reference Short starts at the ship; ours starts where the rope came from (prisoners picking
  oakum, with the real daily amount), then the caulkers, then today's welded steel ships.
- The coloured thing follows the story: the rope and oakum (tan), the pitch (black-brown, shown hot with orange
  steam), then the steel and paint.
