# Your iron used to live on the stove

In the eighteen hundreds, your iron was a solid lump of metal. And you heated it on the stove.

It was called a sad iron. "Sad" was an old word for solid. You set a few of them on the hot stove at once. When one was hot, you grabbed it with a thick cloth. Its handle was metal too, and it could burn you. You pressed it over your shirt, back and forth. Soon, it cooled down. So you put it back, and grabbed the next hot one.

In eighteen seventy-one, a woman named Mary Potts made it easier. Her irons shared one wooden handle that clipped on and off. You clipped it onto whichever iron was hot. No more burned hands.

Today, you plug in an electric iron. A wire inside its flat bottom heats up. A little dial sets how hot. A tiny switch inside turns the heat off when it's hot enough, and back on when it cools. You pour water into a tank. It drips onto the hot plate, and turns to steam. The steam puffs out of little holes, and softens the creases.

Back then, a stove full of hot iron lumps. Now? One plug.

## Why it is built this way

- Template (RULES.md): hook on the surprise (the iron was a lump of metal heated on the stove), the before step by
  step with "you" (heat several, grab with a cloth, press, swap), the turning point (Mary Potts' clip-on handle,
  1871), the now step by step (heating wire, dial, thermostat, steam), one closing line.
- Our own topic (not in the reference Shorts).
- Mary Potts is named (a real inventor) but drawn only as a plain stick figure, never a likeness.
