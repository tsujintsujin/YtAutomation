# Your barber used to drain your blood

Feeling sick in the sixteen hundreds? You went to the barber. And he cut into your arm.

Back then, doctors thought your body was full of four liquids. Too much blood, and you got sick. So the cure was to let some out. The barber did the cutting, right next to the haircuts and shaves.

You sat down and gripped a wooden pole. Squeezing it made the veins in your arm swell up. He tied a strip of cloth tight around your upper arm. Then he took a small, sharp blade and cut into a vein. Your blood ran out into a metal bowl. He let it run until he thought you'd lost enough. Then he wrapped your arm in a white bandage. That's probably why barber poles have red and white stripes. The red for blood. The white for bandages. And it didn't cure much. It often left people weaker.

Today, nobody drains your blood to cure a cold. But a nurse still takes a little, to test it. She ties a stretchy band around your arm. She wipes the spot clean. A thin needle slips into the vein. A few small tubes fill up. Off comes the band, and out comes the needle. A cotton ball, a little pressure, and a bandage. Then a lab checks your blood for what's really wrong.

Back then, a blade and a bowl at the barber. Now? A tiny needle, and a few small tubes.

## Why it is built this way

- Template (RULES.md): hook on the shock (the barber cuts you), why they did it, the before step by step with
  "you", the barber pole as the everyday thing that hides it, the now step by step, one closing line.
- Our own angle: the reference Short explains the pole; ours walks through the whole bleeding at the barber, then
  today's blood test.
- The pole story is told as "probably": the stripes as blood and bandages is a common explanation, not proven.
- Blood is drawn plainly (a flat red stream into a bowl), never gory.
