# A key in your mouth, and a crowd watching

In the seventeen hundreds, a toothache meant a stranger shoving a metal key in your mouth. And then he turned it.

There were no dentists like we have now. Tooth-pullers went from town to town instead. They picked market days, when the square was full of people. So you sat down right there, in front of everyone. Nothing to numb it. Nothing at all.

His tool looked like a big old door key. He slid it into your mouth sideways. A little metal claw on the end hooked around your bad tooth. Then he turned the handle, the way you'd open a lock. Your tooth twisted and twisted until it came loose. Or it snapped. That happened a lot. Sometimes your jaw cracked along with it. And the whole crowd? They watched the entire thing.

Then came eighteen forty-four. A dentist named Horace Wells breathed in laughing gas, and a friend pulled one of his teeth. Afterward, he said it hurt less than a pin prick.

Today it's a whole different story. The dentist takes an X-ray first, to see the roots. One small shot numbs the whole area. Gauze goes in behind the tooth, so it can't slip down your throat. A thin tool slides in beside the tooth and wiggles it loose. Then pliers called forceps rock it side to side and pull it straight out. You bite down on gauze to stop the bleeding. And you go home that same day.

Back then, a key, a crowd, and no numbing. Now? One shot. Then home.

## Why it is built this way

- Template (RULES.md): hook mid-action, the before step by step with "you", the turning point (1844), the now step
  by step, one closing line that puts both side by side.
- The hook is two pictures: the toothache, then the key going in. "And then he turned it" is the first shock.
- Every before step is a picture you can see: the road, the market, the stool, the key, the claw, the twist.
- The last two lines mirror each other: the market crowd picture, then the patient walking home.
- The last line ends the video. No outro.
