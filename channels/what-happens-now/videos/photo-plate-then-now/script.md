# A photo meant freezing for a whole minute

In the eighteen forties, getting your photo taken meant sitting perfectly still. Sometimes for close to a minute.

First, the photographer polished a silver-coated copper plate until it shone like a mirror. In the dark, he held it over iodine fumes, so it would react to light. Then you sat down. A metal stand behind you held your head still. That's probably one reason nobody smiled. He took the cap off the lens, and you froze. Then he put the cap back on. In a dark room, he held the plate over hot mercury fumes, and your face slowly appeared. Mercury is poisonous. Salt water stopped the picture from changing. He sealed it behind glass, in a little case. And there was only one. It was even backwards, like a mirror.

Today, you tap a button. Light goes through a tiny lens onto a sensor. It's a grid of millions of tiny squares, called pixels. Each one measures how much light hits it. Tiny colored filters let each pixel see red, green, or blue. A chip puts all those numbers together into your photo. It cleans it up and saves it, in less than a second. And you can send copies to anyone.

Back then, a minute of sitting frozen. Now? Tap. Done.

## Why it is built this way

- Template (RULES.md): hook on the surprise (a photo meant freezing for up to a minute), the daguerreotype step by
  step with "you" (plate, fumes, head stand, exposure, mercury, salt, case), the now step by step (lens, sensor,
  pixels, filters, chip), one closing line.
- Our own topic (not in the reference Shorts).
- "Nobody smiled" is told as "probably one reason": the long sit is one of several reasons historians give.
