# Before toilet paper, a sponge on a stick

In ancient Rome, you probably wiped with a sponge on a stick. And it was probably shared.

After you used it, you rinsed it in a bucket of vinegar. Then you left it for the next person.

Hundreds of years later, people grabbed whatever was around. Leaves. Moss. Hay. Even snow. On farms, a dried corncob did the job. Then printing made paper cheap. So people tore pages out of old newspapers and books.

In eighteen fifty-seven, an American named Joseph Gayetty sold the first paper made just for wiping. Flat sheets, in a pack. And in eighteen ninety, it came on a roll.

Today, toilet paper starts as a tree. The logs are chopped into little chips. The chips are cooked into a mushy soup of wood fibers. It's washed and made white. Then it's sprayed onto a fast-moving screen, and the water drains away. A giant hot drum dries it into a super thin sheet. A blade scrapes it off and crinkles it soft. It's wound onto a roll heavier than a car. Machines rewind it onto cardboard tubes, in long logs. A spinning saw slices the logs into rolls.

Back then, a shared sponge and a corncob. Now? A soft roll on the wall.

## Why it is built this way

- Template (RULES.md): hook mid-action (the sponge on a stick), the before in order (Rome, then anything at hand,
  then old paper, then the first wiping paper and the roll), the now step by step (how a roll is made), one
  closing line.
- Our own angle: the reference Short jumps from 1857 to Roman pottery; ours goes in time order and adds how toilet
  paper is made today.
- The Roman sponge is told as "probably": historians still argue about how it was used and whether it was shared.
- Nothing crude is shown: people sit fully clothed, nobody is shown wiping.
